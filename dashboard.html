How this table is built

Methodology and scoring, from one answer to one cell

1. What the table shows

Each row is one state, grouped under its geopolitical zone. Each of the ten columns is one ESRA dimension: the nine used by the NEVER Election Security Risk Assessment, plus gender-based risk as a separate cross-cutting lens.

A cell is a share of answers, not a count and not a probability. It says: of every answer monitors in that state gave to the questions in that dimension, this is the percentage that raised a warning. The PESEA column (Pre-Election Security Environment Assessment) is how many monitor reports the row rests on. Incidents is how many violence records the log holds for that state under the current filters.

2. Where the numbers come from

  • Monitor answers come from the published PESEA monitor sheet. One row is one monitor report for one LGA in one reporting week. The dashboard currently reads the published reports.
  • Violence records come from the published violence log. One row is one recorded incident, with its state, LGA, type and deaths.
  • Both are read live in the browser from the published sheets. Nothing in the table is typed in by hand.
  • The year, date-range and state slicers at the top apply to both. A filtered table is therefore not the all-Nigeria table: it only counts reports and incidents inside the selection.

3. One answer becomes one number

The monitor checklist is made of Yes/No questions. The dashboard scores 38 of them, the ones that describe a risk to the election environment. The rest of the checklist records who the monitor is, where they worked and how the visit went.

Half the checklist is written the reassuring way round, so the answer that warns is not always Yes:

  • 26 questions warn on Yes. Example: “Are political actors using words that could incite violence or tension?” Yes is the warning.
  • 12 questions warn on No. Example: “Are security agencies present in the area?” No is the warning. “Have political campaigns been peaceful?” No is the warning.

Each question is tagged once, in code, with the answer that counts as a warning. That tag is the only place direction is set, so a reassuring question can never be scored as if Yes meant danger. A warning answer scores 1. The other answer scores 0.

A blank is not a No. If a monitor leaves a question empty, that answer is dropped: it counts in neither the top nor the bottom of the fraction. This is why cells do not all rest on the same number of answers.

4. From answers to a cell

cell % = (warning answers ÷ answers given) × 100

The result is rounded to a whole number. Worked example, the Political column for the FCT:

Political questionWarningsAnswers
Party disputes25
Inciting speech45
Campaign unrest06
Zoning grievance56
Thugs hired26
Total1328

13 ÷ 28 = 0.464, so the cell reads 46.

The dimension cell is a pooled share, not an average of its signals. Averaging the five percentages above would give 47. Pooling gives 46. Pooling is used because it keeps each answer counting once: a question with six answers should carry a little more weight than one with five, and a question with many blanks should not count as heavily as a fully answered one.

The same pooled rule applies to every dimension cell, to the zone rows and to the overall state score used for ranking.

5. Read the PESEA column with the percentage

PESEA is the number of monitor reports, not the number of people asked. Each report answers up to 38 scored questions, so 6 reports can be up to 228 answers.

  • A cell can read 100 on very little. Kano’s political cell is 5 warnings out of 5 answers, from a single report. It is a real answer, but it is one monitor’s week.
  • The watch strip above ranks only states with 3 or more reports. The table keeps every state so the whole map stays visible, and the zone row shows the total reports behind it.
  • Two cells showing 67 can rest on 3 answers or on 30. The colour is the same; the evidence is not. Hover a cell to see both counts.

Four steps, not a smooth ramp. Neighbouring cells at 70 and 95 stay distinguishable, where a continuous gradient collapses everything above 70 into one tone. The number is always printed in the cell, so colour is a second reading and never the only one.

7. Zone rows

The six zone rows use the same arithmetic over a bigger pool: every report from every state in the zone. It is a pooled share, not the average of the state percentages, so a state with nine reports weighs more than a state with one. PESEA and Incidents are added up across the zone.

Zones follow the official order. Inside a zone, states rank by their overall warning share across all 38 questions, with states under 3 reports placed after the rest so a thin sample never heads the list. Every zone starts folded: click a zone to open its states, click it again to fold them away.

8. The nine dimensions, and what sits in each

The nine follow the ESRA structure and keep its numbering. The split of ESRA dimension 2 into security response, community conflict and identity tension follows how the monitor questions actually cluster.

10. What the table does not say

  • It is not a forecast. It measures what monitors recorded in the selected period, not what will happen. A low score is a quiet week, not a guarantee.
  • It does not rank one signal above another. All 38 questions carry the same weight of one answer. Nothing is judged more serious than anything else.
  • It does not measure severity. One armed attack and one threat both enter the violence log as one incident, so Incidents is a frequency, not a harm scale.
  • Silence is not safety. States with no monitor report are absent from the table altogether. Some states with recorded incidents have no PESEA report. That is a collection gap, not zero risk, and it must not block the model: the gap is named in the note under the table instead of being smoothed over.
  • It does not show change over time. The weekly chart above does, as the share of each week’s answers.
  • It does not compare states as equals. The PESEA column is part of the reading. A state with 9 reports and a state with 1 are not yet on the same footing.
  • PESEA and Incidents answer different questions. PESEA is what monitors saw; Incidents is what the violence log recorded. A high PESEA beside low Incidents can mean early warning is running ahead of events, or that monitors are simply more active there. Read them together, not as a contradiction to resolve.

11. Reproducing any cell by hand

  1. Open the published PESEA monitor sheet.
  2. Filter to one state and the reporting window you want.
  3. Take the column for the question you are checking.
  4. Count the answers that match that question’s warning answer, ignoring blanks.
  5. Divide that count by the number of non-blank answers and multiply by 100.

That is exactly what the dashboard does, from the published CSV, in the browser. If a cell and a hand count disagree, the first thing to check is the warning direction of the question, then the blank answers.
